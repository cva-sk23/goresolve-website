Received a legal notice? Take a breath. Read this entire guide before you respond or pay anything. Most bank legal notices are not court documents — understanding which type you have is the critical first step.

Types of Bank Legal Notices — Not All Are Equal

Banks in Chennai send several types of "legal notices" — and they look similar but have very different implications. Identifying the type correctly determines your response:

How to Identify Which Notice You Have Received

1

Check who signed the notice

A demand letter is signed by a lawyer "on behalf of" the bank. A court summons is issued by a court and signed by the court registrar. A DRT notice has the Debt Recovery Tribunal letterhead and seal. A demand letter from a law firm — however threatening it sounds — is not a court order.

2

Look for a court case number

Actual court proceedings have a case number (e.g., "O.A. No. 123/2026" for DRT). A demand letter from a lawyer's office will not have a court case number — it predates any court filing.

3

Check the letterhead carefully

Is it on the bank's letterhead, a law firm's letterhead, or a court's letterhead? Many "notices" are sent on law firm letterheads and appear official — but a law firm letter is not a court document.

4

Check how it was delivered

Genuine court summons are served through a process server or registered post with acknowledgement. WhatsApp "legal notices" and notices slipped under your door are not court documents — they may still be legitimate demand notices, but they carry no court authority.

⚠️ Fake Legal Notices — Common in Chennai

  • Notices sent via WhatsApp — not how genuine court summons are served
  • Notices without a lawyer's Bar Council registration number
  • Notices claiming "FIR will be filed for fraud" for a standard loan default — loan default is civil, not criminal
  • Notices with typos, incorrect court names, or non-existent case numbers
  • Notices demanding payment to a personal account rather than the bank's account

What To Do Based on Notice Type

If It Is a Demand Letter / Lawyer Notice

This is the most common type. It is serious but not an emergency. You have time to respond properly:

  • Do not ignore it — ignoring a demand letter can lead to escalation to actual court proceedings
  • Do not panic-pay the full amount — this is the notice working as intended
  • Use this as an opportunity to initiate OTS settlement negotiations — a demand letter means the bank wants resolution
  • Respond in writing (via your own lawyer or GOresolve) within the stated deadline
  • Your response should acknowledge the outstanding amount and express willingness to negotiate a settlement

If It Is a SARFAESI Section 13(2) Notice

This applies to secured loans (home loan, vehicle loan, business loan with collateral). The bank has classified your account as NPA and is giving you 60 days to repay before taking possession of the asset. This is the most time-sensitive notice type:

  • You have a right to make a representation to the bank within 60 days — use it
  • File a formal response disputing or negotiating — in some cases, a structured representation or negotiation may temporarily slow or influence the recovery process
  • If the bank rejects your representation, you can appeal to the Debt Recovery Appellate Tribunal (DRAT)
  • Get legal help immediately — this type of notice requires professional response

If It Is an Actual DRT Summons or Court Notice

Do not ignore court summons under any circumstances. Ignoring a DRT summons results in an ex-parte decree against you (the court decides entirely in the bank's favour without hearing your side). Consult a DRT advocate immediately and attend all hearings.

Using a Legal Notice as a Settlement Opportunity

Counterintuitively, receiving a demand notice is often the best time to initiate settlement. The bank has just spent money on legal fees — they want resolution. Frequently Asked Questions

I received a legal notice but can't afford to pay the full amount — what do I do?

Respond to the notice in writing, acknowledging the outstanding amount and proposing a One-Time Settlement. A demand notice is often the often becomes a practical opportunity to begin structured settlement discussions for OTS negotiations — the bank has already incurred legal costs and wants a resolution.

Can the bank send me to jail for not paying a loan?

No. Loan default is a civil matter in India. You cannot be jailed for not paying a loan (except in cheque bounce cases under NI Act Section 138, which is a separate criminal provision). Any agent or notice threatening jail for standard loan default is using an misleading intimidation tactic in standard civil loan-default situations.

Do I need a lawyer to respond to a demand notice?

Not necessarily for a standard demand letter. A well-drafted written response from you or with GOresolve's assistance is sufficient. For SARFAESI notices, DRT summons, or court cases, legal representation is strongly recommended.

The notice says I have 7 days — is that really the deadline?

The deadline in demand letters is typically the bank's preferred timeline, not a hard legal cutoff (unlike SARFAESI's 60-day statutory deadline). Responding after the stated deadline is not ideal but is still better than not responding at all. Courts and banks both respond better to borrowers who engage than those who disappear.
